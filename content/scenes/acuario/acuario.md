# Acuario Las Flores — Interior

> Tags: urban, corporate, eco-themed, commercial, surveillance

**District:** Port

## Overview

Acuario Las Flores is a cost-optimized entertainment complex where the illusion of marine conservation is more valuable than the reality. The building is a single-level, warehouse-style structure — a repurposed LW Group logistics depot converted into an "eco-nature experience" with minimal structural investment. The entrance fee is steep, the crowds are steady, and the actual marine life is both scarce and small. What the aquarium lacks in real animals, it compensates for with holographic spectables, interactive media, and a relentless branding campaign that presents every digital fish as a triumph of ecological awareness.

## The Edifice

The building is a squat, rectangular box of prefabricated steel and insulated panels, its exterior painted in a gradient of ocean blues that peel at the seams. The roof is a gentle arc of corrugated composite, designed more for efficient drainage than aesthetic grandeur. A glass facade runs along the front, but it's more for show than for views — the windows are tinted to obscure the contaminated delta beyond, and the few that aren't are angled to reflect the parking lot back at itself.

The parking zone is the most impressive feature: a paved expanse equal in footprint to the aquarium itself, filled with idling school buses, family sedans, and the occasional corporate shuttle. The lot is so large it has its own shuttle service — a fleet of electric carts that ferry visitors from the far rows to the entrance, their recorded announcements extolling the aquarium's commitment to reducing carbon emissions.

The entrance plaza is a covered portico with a ticketing booth and a security checkpoint. The LW Group logo is omnipresent but subtle: etched into the glass, stamped onto the ticket stubs, embroidered onto the staff uniforms. There is no gift shop here — that comes later, at the exit, where visitors cannot avoid it.

## The Visitor Path

Visitors follow a carefully designed linear route through the aquarium. There is no open-plan wandering — the path is funneled by low walls, rope barriers, and directional signage, ensuring everyone passes through every zone in sequence before reaching the restaurant and gift shops at the exit. The layout maximizes dwell time and commercial exposure while minimizing staffing costs.

The ceiling throughout is a grid of exposed I-beams and ventilation ducts, painted matte black to disappear into the shadows. Suspended from this lattice are the holographic projectors — unobtrusive black cylinders that cast their displays onto walls, floors, and specially marked projection zones along the path.

### Entry & Orientation Hall
Immediately inside the entrance, visitors pass through a narrow transition corridor where the illusion begins. The walls are lined with backlit panels displaying LW Group's conservation messaging, interspersed with sponsor plaques at 1.2-metre intervals. A biometric scanner arch logs every visitor before they reach the ticket validation turnstile. The floor here is dry — the damp concrete starts further in.

A large screen at the corridor's end plays the orientation video on loop, produced by LW Group's educational division. The video frames the visit as both entertaining and educational, though the entertainment is the priority.

### The Holographic Reef Tunnel
The first major attraction is a 20-metre-long corridor where the holographic reef display wraps around visitors. Projectors mounted on both walls and the ceiling create the illusion of being submerged in a coral environment. Digital fish swim past at eye level, digital sharks glide overhead, digital rays flutter beneath raised walkway sections. The effect works best when visitors keep moving — those who stop notice the repetition and occasional glitches.

The tunnel is wide enough for two people to walk abreast, with a low central railing that children can lean against. The floor is slightly raised grated metal here, allowing projection effects from below as well. The same sequence loops every 18 minutes. The console controlling it sits in a recessed alcove, staffed by a single attendant who restarts the system when it crashes.

### The Token Exhibits Row
After the holographic tunnel, the path opens slightly into a single-file queue zone where visitors pass a series of small acrylic tanks built into the wall. Each tank is no larger than a household aquarium: clownfish, a small octopus, a school of neon tetras, a sluggish sea turtle under a fake rock. The tanks are immaculately maintained by automated systems — robotic arms clean the glass at night, sensors monitor water quality, automated feeders dispense food.

Each exhibit has an interactive touchscreen panel providing extensive species information. The panels are positioned at adult eye level, forcing parents to lift children or read aloud. None of the panels mention these are the only real animals in the aquarium, or that the tank sizes fall short of welfare recommendations.

### The Touch Pool Station
A mandatory stop where the path widens into a small semicircular area. A shallow, circular basin filled with engineered sea stars, hermit crabs, and anemones occupies the center. The water is warm and slightly murky from constant handling. A sign instructs visitors to wash their hands, but the washing station is tucked in a corner and often missed.

A maintenance robot — a squat, tiled machine with a flexible arm — constantly skims the surface and adjusts the water temperature. Its movements are slow and deliberate, designed to be non-threatening. It does not respond to questions, but children try anyway.

The path continues from the opposite side of the pool, ensuring everyone gets a turn.

### The Holographic Mural Wall
A 12-metre-long projection wall displays rotating educational content — reef ecosystems, marine food chains, conservation messages. The content is produced by LW Group's communications department and subtly reinforces the company's environmental stewardship narrative. The wall is positioned at a natural resting point, with a few benches where parents can sit while children continue exploring the interactive elements.

### Restaurant Court (Exit Approach)
As visitors near the end, the path opens into the restaurant court. There is no bypass — the exit is through the dining area. "Coral Café" dominates the space with its menu of seafood-themed dishes: fish sticks made from textured soy protein, shrimp from mushroom derivatives, ocean water lemonade as a bright blue sports drink. The seating area has tables arranged to face the holographic displays, so diners can still feel they're part of the experience.

The path weaves between tables, creating a maze that naturally directs visitors toward the registers.

### Gift Shop Gauntlet (Final Exit)
The actual exit is through "Conservation Keepers," the gift shop. There is no other way out. The shop is a narrow corridor lined with merchandise — plush marine animals, branded water bottles, educational toys, eco-friendly trinkets, all manufactured by LW Group subsidiaries. The path snakes through display tables, past the registers, and only then to the door.

The shop is staffed by humans at the registers and service robots that restock shelves and clean floors. The robots are programmed to be friendly, their interactions limited to approved scripts. A final biometric scanner at the exit door logs visitors out, completing the data collection cycle.

### School Tour Assembly Area
A designated zone near the entrance where school groups gather. The area has benches, a water fountain, and a large screen playing the same orientation video. Tour guides in branded polo shirts meet groups here, hand out worksheets, and lead them through the path at a controlled pace. The worksheets are designed to be easy, with answers found on the interactive panels.

The guide also distributes branded stickers and temporary tattoos. Every child leaves with something to remember the experience — and the LW Group logo.

## Sensory Impressions

**Sound:** The constant hum of the ventilation system, the quiet whir of maintenance robots, the occasional glitch and restart of the holographic projectors. The recorded narration from the interactive panels, delivered in a cheerful, upbeat tone that belies the sparse reality of the exhibits. The laughter and shouts of children, the murmur of parents, the rhythmic beeping of the service robots as they navigate the crowds. The sound of the gift shop registers ringing up sales.

**Smell:** The faint scent of popcorn and fast food from the restaurant court. The chemical tang of the cleaning products used to maintain the touch pools and the floors. The faint, synthetic ocean scent diffused through the ventilation system, designed to evoke the seaside without the actual salt air. The underlying note of plastic and electronics, the smell of a place where most of what you see is a projection.

**Touch:** The slightly sticky residue on the interactive touchscreens, the result of countless fingers pressing the same buttons. The smooth, cool surface of the acrylic barriers that separate visitors from the few real exhibits. The textured fabric of the branded merchandise in the gift shop, the plastic sheen of the holographic display floor. The occasional brush against a service robot as it navigates the crowd, its surface warm from continuous operation.

**Sight:** The shifting colors of the holographic reef tunnel wrapping around visitors, their brightness a stark contrast to the dim lighting of the path. The teal glow of the interactive panels mounted along the route. The fluorescent lighting spilling from the restaurant court at the far end, the bright retail lights of the gift shop exit corridor pulling you forward. The LW Group logo, everywhere — on sponsor plaques at 1.2m intervals, on the maintenance robot casing, on the Coral Café menu boards. The linear path stretching ahead, funneled by low walls and rope barriers, with no option to turn back or wander.
