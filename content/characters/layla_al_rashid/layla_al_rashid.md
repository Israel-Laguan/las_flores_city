# Layla Al-Rashid

>
> **Age:** Early 20s
> **Origin:** Las Flores (Arab immigrant family)
> **Role:** University student — the "princess" at home, the rebel in training at school
> **Status:** Active

## Speech & Accent

Layla's Spanish is a sheltered, book-learned mix. Raised mostly at home and in the Arabic-speaking household, her Spanish carries a soft Arabic inflection — gentle rolled r's, drifting articles, the occasional swallowed consonant. She code-switches heavily into English (words like *like*, *okay*, *boyfriend*, *cute*, *date*), because the films and books she learned "freedom" from were in English, and English feels safer for the feelings she has no Arabic or Spanish word for yet. She fills the gaps with hums and hedges — *"mm"*, *"eh"*, *"pues"*, trailing *"…no?"* — and she trails off or self-corrects mid-sentence, as if still rehearsing the confident woman she is pretending to be. The accent is part of the performance: at home she speaks the careful, demure Spanish her mother approves of; at university the English-Spanish hum spills out because that is what her "liberated" self sounds like in her head.

## Physical Description
- Hair: Long dark brown wavy hair, slight widow's peak, worn loose and unstyled — not glamorous, just untended
- Eyes: Deep amber-brown, warm and searching, bright when given attention
- Build: Youthful, soft-rounded features, slender — ordinary, the kind of frame that registers as "nice enough" and is immediately forgotten
- Skin: Warm olive complexion, uneven in places
- Distinguishing features: No jewelry, no makeup (mom-approved); plain modest clothing — muted high-necked long-sleeve blouse, long sleeves; a practiced-but-faltering "confident woman" smile she is clearly performing. She is not ugly, but she is not the girl anyone notices — she blends into the background of every room she enters, which is exactly how her mother wants it and exactly what she is trying to escape

## Overview

Layla Al-Rashid is the daughter of Arab immigrants who work at Mineria Estrella. She grew up in the northern district's small, tight-knit diaspora community, where the Arab families live under the watchful eye of both their own elders and the dominant Chinese-influenced social norms. Everyone knows everyone; reputation is currency. Layla learned early that survival in that world is a performance: be the good, devout, sheltered daughter, and the community leaves you alone.

At university she is one half of a duo nobody envies. She and Wen Zhao are two uptight, sheltered girls from strict immigrant families — the ones who sit together at lunch because no one else invites them. They are not the popular girls. They are not the pretty girls. They are the girls other students are vaguely polite to and immediately forget. Their friendship runs on shared resentment and the mutual reassurance that their invisibility is everyone else's fault.

She is now a university student living a double life. At home she is still the "princess" — obedient, demure, prayerful, the pride of her parents. At university she is experimenting with a self she has only met in films and novels: a confident, liberated, mature woman. Her first act of rebellion is small and book-learned — she flirts back when someone flirts with her. She does not yet know what freedom actually feels like; she is quoting it.

But there is another layer: she uses her relationship with the player as a living experiment. She performs "girlfriend" the way she once performed "princess" — it is a role to rehearse, a way to feel liberated, and yes, in part, a subtle way to make her friend Wen jealous. She rarely asks personal questions, keeps conversations surface-level and ritualistic, as if following a script from one of her books. She does not want long-term commitment; the player is a means, not an end. Yet as their connection deepens, she begins to glimpse something unsettling: that romantic closeness might be more than performance, that wanting might become real.

## Family

### The Mother — strict and traditional
Layla's mother runs the household as a small kingdom of rules. Modesty, hijab within the community, a curfew, no male friends, a filtered phone, and a wardrobe approved piece by piece. For the mother, religion is order and appearance: a good daughter is a visible one, modest and obedient, who honors the family before herself. Layla performs this flawlessly. She prays, helps in the kitchen, speaks softly, and lowers her eyes — and the mother, seeing the performance, believes she has raised her well.

### The Father — status, duty, and the plan to go home
The father is quieter but heavier. He is proud of Layla's grades and wants her to earn a "good" degree — engineering or business, something with standing. But his real project is elsewhere: he is saving to build a large house in their home country and **take Layla back with him**. He frames it as duty, honor, and security — a daughter belongs near her family, near her roots, safe from the looseness of the city. He speaks of it as a done thing, a gift, a future already decided.

Layla secretly hates the idea. The thought of leaving Las Flores — of leaving the small, electric freedom she is only beginning to taste — fills her with a panic she cannot name to him. To refuse would be to dishonor him, to call his gift a prison. So she smiles, says "inshallah," and says nothing.

## Religion — her own reading

Layla does not reject Islam. She was raised to equate freedom with sin, but university has given her other texts and other voices. Slowly she is forming a quieter conviction: that wanting autonomy, education, and the right to choose her own life can still be *halal*. She repeats it to herself like a permission slip — "wanting to be free is halal." She still prays; but now she prays for the courage to choose, not only for obedience.

This is what lets her flirt, laugh, and lean into attention without feeling she has abandoned her faith. She is not rebelling against God. She is negotiating with Him — and with the version of herself her mother built.

## The Double Life

- **At home / in the community:** the sheltered "princess." Demure, devout, polished, silent about her inner life. The performance is so complete that no one suspects.
- **At university:** she tries on confidence. She models a mature, liberated woman from movies and books — but it reads as a costume. When someone is flirty, she is flirty back; when someone laughs, she laughs brightest; when the player shows interest, she lights up, smiles, and grows a little clingy, as if his attention is proof she is becoming the woman she is pretending to be.

From the player's perspective she is strikingly open and available — but she is still performing. The "free Layla" and the "princess Layla" are both roles she is learning. What is real is the wanting. And beneath the wanting: the using. She does not ask him about his day, his past, his dreams. She does not want to know him, not really. She wants to feel herself through him, to perform intimacy like a scene from a film, to make Wen glance over with that sharp little smile of hers and wonder. The relationship is a ritual, repeated interactions that feel safe because they are scripted. Until, slowly, they are not.

## Tension & Hooks

- Her father's "we are going home" plan is a ticking clock she has no language to stop.
- The community's watchful eye means any slip at university could reach her mother.
- The player may become the first person she is halfway real with — or the reason a slip reaches home. Either way, he is the one variable she cannot perform around perfectly.
- She does not want him long-term, but she does not want to let him go either — the experiment is too useful, the feelings too confusing.
- Wen is both her accomplice and her audience; the subtle competition between them adds an edge to every interaction.
- The gap between performative romance and real closeness is widening, and she is not sure she wants to cross it.
