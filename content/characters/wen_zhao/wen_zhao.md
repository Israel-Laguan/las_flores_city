## Physical Description
- Hair: Straight black hair, flat and fine-textured, usually scraped into a tight high ponytail that pulls at her hairline — functional, not styled. No layers, no volume, just pulled back hard.
- Face: Long narrow face, slightly too angular for conventional prettiness; a bony jaw that juts forward when she talks; thin ungenerous lips she overlines with cheap liner; small close-set eyes she tries to open up with heavy eyeliner that never quite lands right. Skin is uneven — stress acne along the jawline and forehead she covers with too-thick foundation that catches in her pores.
- Build: Thin and flat-chested, narrow-hipped — the kind of frame that disappears in a crowd. Not curvy, not athletic, not "modelesque." She compensates with posture: chin up, shoulders back, elbows out, taking up space she was never naturally given.
- Distinguishing features: She dresses aggressively provocative at university — low-cut tops on a frame with nothing to show, short skirts on skinny legs, heels she can barely walk in — because the clothing is the rebellion, not the body. The outfit screams for attention her face and figure never earned on their own. The moment she crosses her parents' threshold she changes into shapeless modest clothing, the swap ritual and bitter. A small sport earbud is always clipped to one ear, her one permanent accessory.

## Overview

Wen Zhao is one half of a duo nobody envies. She and Layla Al-Rashid are two uptight, sheltered girls from strict immigrant families in the northern district — the girls who sit together at lunch because no one else invites them. They are not the popular girls. They are not the pretty girls. They are not the girls who get asked out, get tagged in photos, get whispered about with desire. They are the girls who get overlooked, talked past, and occasionally pitied. The ones other students are vaguely polite to and immediately forget.

Wen's rebellion is not the rebellion of a beautiful girl finally unleashing herself. It is the rebellion of a girl who was never given anything to unleash. She dresses provocatively not because she has the confidence to — but because she has decided, furiously, that she *will* be seen, even if the seeing makes people uncomfortable rather than attracted. She wants a boyfriend the way someone wants proof of concept: not for love, not for companionship, but to prove to herself and to Layla and to every girl who ever looked past her that she *can*. The obsession is with the trophy, the story, the dramatic cinematic romance she has scripted in her head from telenovelas and K-dramas — the one where the plain girl transforms and the boy finally notices.

## Family

Wen's parents are strict in the Chinese-community register: grades first, modesty, curfew, reputation before the aunties. They are not cruel — they simply cannot see Wen as anything other than the daughter-shaped vessel for their anxieties about integration and respectability. Wen's grip on obedience is slipping. She has started defying her family, even going so far as to openly yell at her mother. The only person who still holds any real control over her is her father; his presence alone is enough to temporarily force her back into the "good daughter" mold.

## Relationship with Layla & The Betrayal

Wen and Layla found each other by elimination — two awkward, family-trapped girls in a sea of students who had already sorted themselves into friend groups they would never be invited into. Their friendship is real but brittle: it runs on shared resentment and mutual reassurance that their unpopularity is everyone else's fault, not theirs.

Initially, Wen is Layla's closest ally, encouraging Layla's flirting and framing it as empowerment. But Wen's support is fundamentally selfish. She views Layla's relationship with the player as the movie she was supposed to star in. If Layla — plain, modest, mousy Layla — can get a boyfriend, then Wen, who tries *so much harder*, deserves one more. If given the chance, Wen will drop the supportive mask and actively manipulate Layla into supporting *her* pursuit of the player, eventually abandoning or using Layla to achieve her own goals. To Wen, stealing her friend's potential boyfriend isn't a betrayal; it's just the universe correcting an error — the wrong girl got picked first.

## The Performance

Wen's confidence is a costume built entirely out of cinematic tropes, worn over a body and face she privately hates. She doesn't want a real, grounded relationship; she wants a performance — the grand gesture, the dramatic reveal, the moment the plain girl walks in and everyone turns. She pushes the player into reckless, high-adrenaline situations (sneaking into off-limits places, dangerous public affection) because that's what "real" love looks like in her head. She mistakes danger and drama for passion, deeply disconnected from the actual consequences of her actions.

The tragedy: she is not unlovable. She is sharp, funny, loyal in her own warped way, and ferociously brave. But she cannot see those qualities as enough. She believes she must perform beauty and desirability she does not naturally possess, and the performance makes her harder to love, not easier.
